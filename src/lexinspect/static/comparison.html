Different numbers deserve an explanation.

Gunning Fog

16.47 vs 16.26

Both tools find 53 words with three or more syllables. LexInspect counts 52 as Fog-complex words: it excludes Measuring, because measure has only two syllables.

Our implementation excludes words that reach three syllables only through an -es, -ed or -ing ending. Using all 53 polysyllabic words produces precisely Text Inspector’s score.

53 complex words16.47
52 complex words16.26

This strongly supports a counting-rule explanation for this sample. We did not inspect Text Inspector’s internal implementation.

voc-D

200.00 vs 245.48

This remains unresolved. voc-D uses random subsamples and curve fitting. LexInspect uses seeded sampling, so the same input produces the same result; Text Inspector’s exact fitting settings were not verified.

Text Inspector describes scores as typically between 0 and 200. That does not prove it imposes a cap. We would need a broader test set to establish whether sampling, fitting bounds or another implementation choice explains the difference.

Academic vocabulary

12.44% vs 13.402%

LexInspect finds 26 AWL tokens among 194 words, across 26 families. Text Inspector’s detailed AWL view was locked, so we could not inspect which words or denominator produced its percentage. This difference does not establish a bug in either tool.

CEFR vocabulary

23.68% vs 17.526% at B1

LexInspect uses CEFR-J and Octanove; Text Inspector uses the English Vocabulary Profile. Different vocabulary resources and classification rules mean the percentages are not expected to be identical.

LexInspect’s B2 estimate describes the level at which this passage reaches 95% lexical coverage. It is not an overall writing-proficiency score. We excluded Text Inspector’s locked C2 label from the comparison because it also appeared before we entered the passage.

Schools increasingly use community gardens to connect classroom learning with practical experience. In these spaces, pupils observe how plants respond to changes in light, temperature and water. They record their observations, compare results and explain why some crops develop more successfully than others. This process encourages careful reasoning rather than the simple memorisation of scientific facts.

However, establishing a garden requires more than enthusiasm. Teachers must allocate time, obtain suitable equipment and ensure that every child can participate safely. Limited funding may affect the range of activities, while poor weather can interrupt planned lessons. Cooperation with local residents can provide additional expertise and help schools maintain the garden during holidays.

The educational benefits also extend beyond science. Measuring growing areas offers opportunities to practise mathematics, and writing reports develops the ability to communicate evidence clearly. Working together may strengthen social relationships, although these outcomes depend on how responsibilities are shared. A successful programme therefore needs clear objectives and regular evaluation. Schools should examine whether the activities improve understanding, include pupils with different needs and remain manageable for staff. When these conditions are met, a modest garden can become a valuable resource for sustained learning.

What this test cannot tell us

One passage cannot establish general accuracy. It contains no digits, contractions, abbreviations or hyphenated compounds. Matching totals can also hide different word-level decisions. Detailed vocabulary comparisons were limited by guest access.

Open evidence. Clear limits.

The useful next steps are to validate voc-D across varied passages, reconcile academic-word matches when detailed access is available, and test segmentation edge cases. Those checks have not yet been completed.
